## 115.1 Noetherian Rings

### Theorem 115.1.1 (Ascending Chain Condition)
Every Noetherian ring satisfies the ascending chain condition on ideals.

**Proof:** Let $(R, +, \cdot)$ be a Noetherian ring. By definition, a ring is Noetherian if its ideal lattice is finitely generated, or equivalently, if it satisfies the ascending chain condition on ideals.

Consider an ascending chain of ideals:
$$I_1 \subseteq I_2 \subseteq I_3 \subseteq \cdots$$

Since $R$ is Noetherian, the set $\{I_1, I_2, I_3, \dots\}$ has a maximal element (by definition of Noetherian). Let $I_n$ be this maximal element. Then for all $m > n$, we have $I_m \subseteq I_n$. But we also have $I_n \subseteq I_{n+1}$, so $I_n = I_{n+1}$.

By induction, $I_n = I_{n+1} = I_{n+2} = \cdots$, so the chain stabilizes after at most $n$ steps. ∎

### Theorem 115.1.2 (Bézout's Identity for Noetherian Domains)
In a Noetherian integral domain $R$, every finitely generated ideal is principal.

**Proof:** Let $I = (a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n)$ be a finitely generated ideal in a Noetherian integral domain $R$.

Since $R$ is Noetherian, we can construct a principal ideal $I$ such that it contains all the elements $a_1, \dots, a_n$. We know that $(a_1) \subseteq I$, $(a_2) \subseteq I$, and so on. By the Noetherian property, there exists a principal ideal $J$ such that $(a_1, \dots, a_n) \subseteq J$ and $J$ is generated by a single element $d$, i.e., $J = (d)$.

Since $a_1, \dots, a_n \in (d)$, we have $d \in (a_1, \dots, a_n)$, so $d = \sum_{i=1}^n r_i a_i$ for some $r_1, \dots, r_n \in R$. Thus $(d) \subseteq (a_1, \dots, a_n)$, so $(d) = (a_1, \dots, a_n)$. ∎

### Theorem 115.1.3 (Hilbert's Basis Theorem)
If $R$ is a Noetherian ring, then $R[x]$ is also a Noetherian ring.

**Proof:** Let $R$ be a Noetherian ring. We need to show that $R[x]$ is Noetherian, i.e., that every ideal in $R[x]$ is finitely generated.

Let $I$ be an ideal in $R[x]$. For each polynomial $f(x) = a_0 + a_1x + \dots + a_nx^n \in I$, we can associate an integer $d(f) = \max\{n : a_n \neq 0\}$ (the degree of $f$).

Define $J = \{a_0 : f(x) = a_0 + a_1x + \dots + a_nx^n \in I, n = 0\}$, which is the set of constant polynomials in $I$. Since $R$ is Noetherian, $J$ is finitely generated, say $J = (b_1, \dots, b_k)$.

For $n \geq 1$, let $P_n = \{a_n : f(x) = a_0 + a_1x + \dots + a_nx^n \in I, a_n \neq 0\}$. Each $P_n$ is a subset of $R$, and since $R$ is Noetherian, each $P_n$ is finitely generated.

Let $I_n$ be the ideal generated by all $a_n$ such that there exists a polynomial $f(x) \in I$ with $d(f) = n$. Then $I_n$ is finitely generated for each $n$.

We can now express any ideal $I$ as $I = J + I_1x + I_2x^2 + \dots$, where $J = (b_1, \dots, b_k)$.

To show that $I$ is finitely generated, we need to show that there exist finitely many polynomials $f_1, \dots, f_m \in I$ such that $I = (f_1, \dots, f_m)$.

Let $d_0, \dots, d_m$ be degrees such that $I_{d_j} \neq (0)$. Then we can choose polynomials $f_j \in I$ with $d(f_j) = d_j$ such that the leading coefficients of $f_j$ generate $I_{d_j}$ for $j = 0, \dots, m$.

Then $I = (f_1, \dots, f_m)$. ∎

## 115.2 Prime Ideals

### Theorem 115.2.1 (Prime Ideal Definition)
An ideal $P$ in a ring $R$ is prime if and only if:
1. $P \neq R$, and
2. For any $a, b \in R$, if $ab \in P$, then $a \in P$ or $b \in P$.

**Proof:** This is the definition of a prime ideal. The "if and only if" is immediate from the definition. ∎

### Theorem 115.2.2 (Prime Implies Proper)
Every prime ideal $P$ in a ring $R$ is a proper ideal ($P \neq R$).

**Proof:** Suppose, for contradiction, that $P = R$. Then $1 \in P$. Since $1 \in P$, for any $a \in R$, we have $a = a \cdot 1 \in P$, so $P = R$.

But if $1 \in P$, then for any $a, b \in R$, we have $ab = a \cdot b \in P$. Since $P$ is prime, either $a \in P$ or $b \in P$. But this doesn't lead to a contradiction directly.

Instead, note that if $P = R$, then $1 \in P$. But by the definition of a prime ideal, if $P = R$, then for any $a, b \in R$, we would have $ab \in P$ (since all products are in $R$), and we would require $a \in P$ or $b \in P$.

To show $P \neq R$, note that if $1 \in P$, then for any $a \in R$, we have $a = a \cdot 1 \in P$, so $P = R$. Thus, if $P$ is prime, we must have $1 \notin P$, so $P \neq R$. ∎

### Theorem 115.2.3 (Krull's Theorem on Maximal Ideals)
Every proper ideal $I$ in a ring $R$ is contained in a maximal ideal $M$, which is prime.

**Proof:** Consider the set of all proper ideals containing $I$: $\mathcal{S} = \{J : I \subseteq J \subsetneq R\}$.

Since $I$ itself is in $\mathcal{S}$, $\mathcal{S}$ is non-empty. By Zorn's Lemma, $\mathcal{S}$ has a maximal element $M$.

We claim $M$ is a maximal ideal in $R$. Suppose $M \subseteq K \subsetneq R$. Then $M$ would not be maximal in $\mathcal{S}$, contradicting the maximality of $M$.

To show $M$ is prime, we need to show that if $ab \in M$, then $a \in M$ or $b \in M$.

Suppose $ab \in M$ but $a \notin M$ and $b \notin M$. Then the ideals $M$ and $(a) + M$ are distinct proper ideals containing $M$.

Consider the ideals $M$ and $(a) + M$. Since $M$ is maximal, $(a) + M = R$, so $1 = ma + m'$ for some $m, m' \in M$.

Then $b = 1 \cdot b = (ma + m')b = mab + m'b$. Since $ab \in M$ and $m', M \in M$, we have $b \in M$, contradicting $b \notin M$.

Therefore, if $ab \in M$, we must have $a \in M$ or $b \in M$. Thus $M$ is prime. ∎

## 115.3 Regular Elements and Prime Ideals

### Theorem 115.3.1 (Regular Elements)
In an integral domain $R$, an element $a \in R$ is regular (i.e., a non-zero divisor) if and only if $a$ is not a zero divisor.

**Proof:** In an integral domain, $ab = 0$ implies $a = 0$ or $b = 0$. Thus, if $a \neq 0$, then for any $b \neq 0$, we have $ab \neq 0$. So $a$ is not a zero divisor.

Conversely, if $a$ is a non-zero divisor, then $ab = 0$ implies $a = 0$ or $b = 0$. If $a \neq 0$, then for any $b \neq 0$, we have $ab \neq 0$. Thus $a$ is not a zero divisor. ∎

### Theorem 115.3.2 (Associated Primes)
In a Noetherian domain $R$, every non-zero element belongs to a finite number of prime ideals.

**Proof:** Let $a \in R$ be a non-zero element. The set of prime ideals containing $a$ is $\{P : a \in P\}$.

Since $R$ is Noetherian, we can use the primary decomposition theorem. For any non-zero $a \in R$, we can write:

$$(a) = Q_1 \cap Q_2 \cap \dots \cap Q_n$$

where each $Q_i$ is a primary ideal, and $P_i = \text{rad}(Q_i)$ are the associated primes of $a$.

Since the decomposition is finite, there are finitely many primes $P_i$ such that $a \in P_i$. ∎

## 115.4 Commutativity and Noetherian Rings

### Theorem 115.4.1 (Commutativity of Noetherian Rings)
Not every Noetherian ring is commutative. However, every commutative Noetherian ring has the property that every ideal is finitely generated.

**Proof:** There are many examples of non-commutative Noetherian rings. For instance, consider the ring of linear operators on a finite-dimensional vector space.

However, for any commutative Noetherian ring $R$, every ideal is finitely generated by definition. This is a key property that distinguishes Noetherian rings from other classes of rings. ∎

## 115.5 Localization

### Theorem 115.5.1 (Localization of Noetherian Rings)
If $R$ is a Noetherian ring and $S \subseteq R$ is a multiplicative subset, then $S^{-1}R$ is a Noetherian ring.

**Proof:** Let $S \subseteq R$ be a multiplicative subset of a Noetherian ring $R$. We need to show that $S^{-1}R$ is Noetherian.

Let $I_1 \subseteq I_2 \subseteq \dots$ be an ascending chain of ideals in $S^{-1}R$. For each $I_n$, define $J_n = \{s \in S : s^{-1}r \in I_n \text{ for some } r \in R\}$.

Since $R$ is Noetherian, the chain $J_1 \subseteq J_2 \subseteq \dots$ stabilizes after finitely many steps. Thus, the chain $I_1 \subseteq I_2 \subseteq \dots$ also stabilizes. ∎

### Theorem 115.5.2 (Krull Dimension)
If $R$ is a Noetherian ring, then the Krull dimension of $R$ is finite.

**Proof:** The Krull dimension of $R$, denoted $\dim(R)$, is the supremum of the lengths of chains of prime ideals in $R$.

Since $R$ is Noetherian, the set of prime ideals in $R$ is well-ordered. This implies that the length of any chain of prime ideals in $R$ is finite.

Therefore, the Krull dimension of $R$ is finite. ∎
