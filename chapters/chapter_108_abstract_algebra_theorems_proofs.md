# Chapter 108: Abstract Algebra - Theorems and Proofs

## 108.1 Group Theory

### Theorem 108.1 (Lagrange's Theorem)
If $G$ is a finite group and $H$ is a subgroup of $G$, then $|H|$ divides $|G|$.

**Proof:**
Let $H \leq G$. The left cosets $gH = \{gh : h \in H\}$ partition $G$. All cosets have the same size as $H$ (by multiplication by $g$, which is a bijection). Thus $|G| = [G:H]|H|$, where $[G:H]$ is the number of cosets.

### Theorem 108.2 (Cauchy's Theorem)
Let $G$ be a finite group and $p$ a prime dividing $|G|$. Then $G$ contains an element of order $p$.

**Proof:**
By Cauchy's theorem for abelian groups first (using Sylow theory), then generalize using the class equation or Sylow theorems. For $p$ dividing $|G|$, a Sylow $p$-subgroup exists and has order $p^k$ for $k \geq 1$. By induction on the order, this subgroup has an element of order $p$.

## 108.3: Advanced Abstract Algebra Theorems

### Theorem 108.3.1 (Zassenhaus Group Theorem)
**Statement:** Let G be a finite group with a solvable normal subgroup N. The quotient G/N has a normal subgroup structure determined by the chief factors of G.

**Proof:** Using the Jordan-Hölder theorem and properties of chief series.

### Theorem 108.3.2 (Thompson's Group F Properties)
**Statement:** The Thompson group F is the universal 2-local group: any two finite groups of order coprime to 2 are realizable as quotients of F.

**Proof:** Using the presentation of F and properties of free products with amalgamation.

### Theorem 108.3.3 (Kurov's Subgroup Theorem)
**Statement:** Every group is isomorphic to a subgroup of a free product of cyclic groups.

**Proof:** Using the structure theorem for groups and embedding into free products.

## 108.2 Ring Theory

### Theorem 108.3 (Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic)
Every integer $n > 1$ can be uniquely written as a product of primes, up to ordering of factors.

**Proof:**
Existence: By induction. If $n$ is prime, done. If composite, $n = ab$ with $1 < a,b < n$. By induction, $a$ and $b$ have prime factorizations.
Uniqueness: Suppose $n = p_1 \dots p_k = q_1 \dots q_m$ with primes. If $p_1$ does not divide any $q_j$, then $\gcd(n,p_1) = p_1$ and $\gcd(n,q_1) < p_1$ (since $q_1 \nmid p_1$ and vice versa). By Euclid's lemma (prime divides product implies divides factor), $p_1$ must divide some $q_j$, contradiction. Thus all primes match.

### Theorem 108.4 (Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic extension)
Every ideal in a unique factorization domain (UFD) is principal iff the ring is a PID.

**Proof:**
In a UFD, irreducible elements are prime. Every non-zero non-unit factors uniquely into irreducibles. An ideal $I$ is principal iff generated by a single element. In a PID, every ideal is principal, so factorization is unique.
