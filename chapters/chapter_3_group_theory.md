# Chapter 3: Group Theory Fundamentals

## 3.1 Group Axioms

### Theorem 3.1: Definition of a Group

**Statement**: A set $G$ with a binary operation $\cdot$ is a group if:

1. **Closure**: For all $a, b \in G$, $a \cdot b \in G$
2. **Associativity**: For all $a, b, c \in G$, $(a \cdot b) \cdot c = a \cdot (b \cdot c)$
3. **Identity**: There exists $e \in G$ such that for all $a \in G$, $e \cdot a = a \cdot e = a$
4. **Inverses**: For each $a \in G$, there exists $a^{-1} \in G$ such that $a \cdot a^{-1} = a^{-1} \cdot a = e$

**Proof**: This is the definition of a group. The four axioms together characterize all group structures. ∎

## 3.2 Group Properties

### Theorem 3.2: Unique Identity and Unique Inverses

**Statement**: In any group $G$:
1. The identity element is unique.
2. The inverse of each element is unique.
3. For any $a, b \in G$, $(a^{-1})^{-1} = a$.

**Proof**:

1. Suppose $e_1$ and $e_2$ are both identities. Then:
   $e_1 = e_1 \cdot e_2 = e_2$ (using identity property)
   Therefore, the identity is unique.

2. Suppose $b_1$ and $b_2$ are both inverses of $a$. Then:
   $b_1 = e \cdot b_1 = a \cdot b_1 \cdot b_2 = a \cdot (b_1 \cdot b_2) = a \cdot b_2 = e \cdot b_2 = b_2$
   Therefore, the inverse is unique.

3. $(a^{-1})^{-1} = a$ because $a^{-1} \cdot a = e$, so the inverse of $a^{-1}$ must be $a$. ∎

### Theorem 3.3: Cancellation Laws

**Statement**: In a group $G$, for all $a, b, c \in G$:
1. If $ab = ac$, then $b = c$ (left cancellation)
2. If $ba = ca$, then $b = c$ (right cancellation)

**Proof**:

1. $ab = ac \implies ab \cdot a^{-1} = ac \cdot a^{-1} \implies b = c$ (using $a^{-1}$ as right inverse)

2. Similarly for right cancellation. ∎

## 3.3 Subgroups

### Theorem 3.4: Subgroup Criterion

**Statement**: Let $H$ be a non-empty subset of a group $G$. Then $H$ is a subgroup of $G$ if and only if for all $a, b \in H$, $ab^{-1} \in H$.

**Proof**:

(⇒) If $H$ is a subgroup, then by the subgroup definition, $H$ is closed under the operation and inverses. So for $a, b \in H$, $b^{-1} \in H$ and $ab^{-1} \in H$.

(⇐) Suppose for all $a, b \in H$, $ab^{-1} \in H$. Since $H$ is non-empty, let $a \in H$. Then $aa^{-1} = e \in H$, so $H$ contains the identity. By induction, all powers $a^n$ are in $H$. Also $a(-1) = a^{-1} \in H$. Therefore $H$ contains inverses. The closure of $ab^{-1}$ implies closure under multiplication and inverses, making $H$ a subgroup. ∎

## 3.4 Lagrange's Theorem

### Theorem 3.5: Lagrange's Theorem for Finite Groups

**Statement**: If $G$ is a finite group and $H$ is a subgroup of $G$, then the order of $H$ divides the order of $G$.

**Proof**:

Consider the left cosets of $H$ in $G$: $aH = \{ah : h \in H\}$ for $a \in G$.

1. If $aH = bH$, then for any $h_1, h_2 \in H$, $ah_1 = bh_2$ implies a bijection between $aH$ and $bH$.
2. The cosets form a partition of $G$.
3. If $|G| = n$ and $|H| = m$, and there are $k$ distinct cosets, then $n = km$.
4. Thus, $m$ divides $n$. ∎

## 3.5 Order of Elements

### Theorem 3.6: Order of Elements

**Statement**: For any element $a$ in a finite group $G$, the order of $a$ divides $|G|$.

**Proof**:

The order of $a$, denoted $|a|$, is the smallest positive integer $n$ such that $a^n = e$.

By Lagrange's Theorem, the subgroup $\langle a \rangle$ generated by $a$ has order $|a|$.

Since $\langle a \rangle \subseteq G$, $|a|$ divides $|G|$.
